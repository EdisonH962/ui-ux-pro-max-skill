# 📦 TINY UNBOXING REEL — STYLE BIBLE & PROMPT KIT

**The one knowledge file for this skill.** It holds the fixed video-prompt blocks, the hero-beat picks, the unboxing shot archetypes, the hands/tools/sound banks, the shot-count math, and one full worked example. Read it before building. **Reuse its wording verbatim — vary only the product, the scale and the shot list.**

> **What this skill makes:** a locked-off-camera, hard-cut macro reel where a scale-accurate miniature replica of a real product gets unboxed by giant adult hands. The scale contrast IS the hook — never let the product read life-sized.

---

## 🎬 Hero beat picks

The hero image is ONE frame, chosen as the single most "tiny vs giant" moment of the whole unbox. **The retail box's own printed art is the star of this niche** — real logo, real cover art, real barcode and rating stickers, all rendered at tiny scale. Never pick a beat that shows the bare, already-unboxed product with no packaging in frame anywhere — that reads as a toy photo, not an unboxing.

- **The reveal** *(default)* — fingers reaching down from the top of frame, hovering just above the still-sealed miniature retail box, not yet touching it. The box's full printed cover art is in crisp focus. This is the anticipation beat and reads the format fastest in a single still.
- **The cut** — a blade mid-slice through the tape seal of the miniature box, box art visible on the near face.
- **The extract** — tweezers lifting the smallest accessory (a cable, a SIM pin, a lace tip) out of its packaging, the opened box still in frame behind it.

Default to **the reveal** when unsure. Only use a bare-product beat (holding the finished item with zero packaging anywhere in frame) for the mandatory HERO STAND shot at the very end of the video's shot list — never for the hero reference image itself.

---

## 🔬 Tweezer-first handling — the precision-tool sells the scale

**Every delicate unwrapping or extraction action uses fine-tipped tweezers, not bare fingers.** A giant hand wielding a tiny precision tool against tiny packaging reads as more convincingly miniature than bare fingertips alone — the tool itself becomes a second scale reference, and the deliberate, careful handling (a real person would use tools this precise on something this small) sells the illusion. This is the single detail most worth over-indexing on for realism.

- **Fingers/hand** are for the big, coarse moves: lifting the whole box, opening the lid, holding or tilting the assembled product at the end.
- **Tweezers** are for everything fine: peeling shrink film, peeling inner plastic wrap, lifting a blister pack from its slot, positioning any small part. Default to tweezers for these even when the archetype below says "fingers" — swap it in unless the user's product genuinely has nothing small enough to warrant it (rare).
- Tools list in the style paragraph should include tweezers in nearly every reel — dropping it entirely is the exception, not the rule.

---

## 📐 Extra scale-contrast techniques — beyond hands and tweezers

Layer these on top of the scale anchor whenever they fit; they're cheap in prompt terms and compound with each other.

- **Crop the hand, never fit it.** The hand entering frame should read as too large to fully fit — cropped by the frame edge, not a whole hand floating with air around it. A fully-visible hand reads as normal-sized; a cropped one reads as gigantic. Already implicit in "reaching down from the top of frame" — keep it that way rather than pulling back to show a whole hand.
- **Skin macro detail.** Mention fingerprint ridges, pores, or fine skin texture in crisp focus at this magnification — it's free realism that also sells "this lens is genuinely macro, not just a close-up of a normal-sized scene."
- **Focus falloff onto the hand itself**, not just the background. At true macro magnification even part of a giant fingertip can fall outside the plane of focus. A hint of this (`the fingertip's ridge lines crisp where it meets the box, softening slightly toward the knuckle`) reads as authentic macro optics.
- **Physical interaction cues.** Let the packaging visibly respond to the hand's touch — cardboard flexing slightly under a fingertip's pressure, plastic film tenting before it tears, the tabletop's wood grain looking coarse and oversized beneath the product. Showing force asymmetry (huge finger, delicate give in tiny material) sells weight and scale together.
- **A found-object scale reference, used sparingly.** A single grain of sawdust, a stray thread, or a speck of dust sitting near the product at a size that makes sense next to it (never staged or commented on) gives the eye a second, mundane scale cue beyond the hand.
- **Deliberate, careful motion in the action line.** Write hand movements as slow and precise ("carefully," "gently," a controlled release) rather than quick — real handling of something this fragile-small would be careful, and unhurried motion reads as heavier/larger than fast motion.

Don't stack all of these in every single shot — one or two per shot is plenty; overloading the sentence with every technique at once reads as a keyword pile, not a photograph.

## 📐 Box orientation — stability rule

**The box must always rest flat on its largest face, never balanced upright on a narrow edge.** A tall, narrow box standing on end (the real proportions of a console or phone box, for instance) has a small, unstable footprint. Video models frequently show this kind of object wobbling, tipping over, or floating unnaturally between hard cuts — it reads as a physics glitch, not a locked-off shot.

This applies even when the real product's retail box genuinely is tall and narrow. Lay it on its largest face (usually the front-cover face or the back face) so it reads as physically stable, the same way it would actually sit on a table before someone picks it up.

- Hero image and every box-still-closed shot (SEAL CUT, LID LIFT): state the orientation explicitly — `the box resting flat on its largest face on the tabletop`.
- If a shot specifically needs the box being picked up, held, or turned in hand, that's fine — instability only becomes a bug when the box is meant to be sitting untouched.
- Never describe a box "standing upright" or "standing on end" unless the user's product genuinely ships in a wide, stable-footed box that stands naturally (rare — most don't).

## 🔒 THE FIXED BLOCKS

### 1. Opener (always first, identical apart from the count words)

```text
A fast-cut {{DURATION}}-second macro miniature unboxing reel made of {{SHOTS_CAPS}} separate shots with {{cuts}} hard cuts. Every shot is a locked-off macro camera — absolutely no push-ins, no pull-backs, no pans, no tilts, no handheld drift, no camera movement of any kind. All motion comes exclusively from the hands and the miniature product moving inside a static frame. Cut hard on every beat, never crossfade or dissolve.
```

### 2. Style paragraph (two lines, always both)

```text
ONE locked photorealistic macro style across all {{shots}} shots, matching the reference image exactly: a hyper-detailed, scale-accurate miniature replica of {{PRODUCT_FULL_NAME}} at {{SCALE}}, its retail packaging reproduced with full realism — real cover art, logo placement, printed text and barcode all rendered at tiny scale — every logo, texture and material on the product itself also rendered with full realism, shot in extreme macro product photography, shallow depth of field with focus falling off even across parts of the hand itself, warm naturally saturated daylight grade with the key light from the upper left casting gentle soft shadows, background a soft neutral out-of-focus blur with no other furniture or objects recognizable.
The same adult hands, cropped by the frame edge and never fully visible, their skin texture and fingerprint ridges rendered in crisp macro detail, same warm honey-toned wooden tabletop with its grain reading coarse and oversized at this scale, and same {{TOOLS_LIST}} recur across the shots. Object continuity must remain exact throughout: {{PRODUCT_CONTINUITY_LIST}}. Only bare adult hands, the miniature product, its packaging and the named tools are ever in frame; the framing never widens past the tabletop.
```

`{{TOOLS_LIST}}` drops any tool the shot list never uses — never name a tool that doesn't appear (same rule as a prop that never appears in a cooking reel). The packaging clause stays in every shot's style paragraph even after the box itself is discarded mid-reel — it describes the locked rendering style, not a claim that the box is still on screen.

### 3. Shot list — the only block written fresh

```text
{{start}}-{{end}} — SHOT {{n}} (look of reference image). {{camera}}. {{entry}}. {{action}}. {{exit or hold}}.
```

Pull `{{camera}}` from §Unboxing shot archetypes below — never invent new camera language mid-project.

### 4. Closer

```text
{{CUTS_CAP}} clean hard cuts, {{shots}} locked-off macro shots, no crossfades, no whip pans, no camera movement of any kind. Natural diegetic sound only — {{SOUND_BANK}}. No background music, no voice-over, no dialogue. No face, no head, no torso, no full body, no person on screen, no distorted or extra fingers. No household furniture, no visible room or walls beyond the soft-blurred background. No on-screen text, no captions, no subtitles, no lettering, no watermark.
```

### 5. Consistency line

```text
{{PRODUCT_SHORT_NAME}} must remain visually identical in scale, colour and detail across every shot.
```

## 🎥 Unboxing shot archetypes

Camera strings are verbatim — reuse them, never paraphrase. Order them to match the product's real unboxing sequence; not every product uses every archetype.

**0 OUTER WRAP PEEL** · when the whole box ships inside its own clear shrink film (common for electronics) · 1.5-2.0 s
`Close macro three-quarter angle on the corner of the box.`
Tweezers find a loose edge or seam of the outer shrink film, peel it back and off the box in one continuous pull, revealing the printed cardboard beneath. If the product's real packaging uses this, it always comes before SEAL CUT — never cut tape through an outer film that hasn't been removed yet.

**1 SEAL CUT** · strong opener (or second shot, after OUTER WRAP PEEL) · 1.5-2.0 s
`Top-down macro close-up on the box's taped seam.`
A blade enters from the right, slices cleanly through the tape along the box edge, withdraws right.

**2 LID LIFT** · mandatory if boxed · 1.5-2.0 s
`Eye-level macro close-up on the box.`
Fingers enter from both sides, lift the lid open, revealing the wrapped product inside. Exit: lid held open or set aside.

**3 FILM PEEL** · when the product itself ships in plastic wrap (distinct from OUTER WRAP PEEL — this is the inner film around the item, not the box) · 1.5-2.0 s
`Close macro three-quarter angle on the wrapped product.`
Tweezers pinch a loose edge of the plastic film and peel it back in one continuous pull, revealing the surface beneath.

**4 TRAY / LINER PULL** · mandatory whenever any accessory, blister pack or cable appears in a later shot · 1.5-2.0 s
`Slight high angle on the open box.`
Both hands lift the inner tray or foam liner clear of the box, revealing the accessory compartments beneath — the blister pack, cable or accessory that a later shot will pick up must be visible sitting in this tray. Exit: tray set to the side. **Never skip this and cut straight to an accessory already free-standing on the table — that reads as the item appearing from nowhere.**

**5 BLISTER EXTRACT** · when a small accessory ships sealed · 1.5-2.0 s
`Close macro on the blister-packed accessory.`
Tweezers enter from the right, carefully extract the item from its slot, withdraw right. This is the archetype for the smallest, fiddliest piece — the one that best proves the scale. The blister pack itself must already be visible from the preceding TRAY / LINER PULL shot; this shot continues from where that one left off, never a fresh unexplained object.

**6 CABLE / ACCESSORY LAY-OUT** · optional, complex unboxes only · 1.2-1.8 s
`Overhead macro close-up on the tabletop.`
Tweezers set each remaining accessory down in a line — cable, adapter, manual — one at a time, releasing each before reaching for the next.

**7 ASSEMBLY / SNAP-IN** · when parts connect (case on phone, insole in shoe, controller battery) · 1.5-2.0 s
`Close macro three-quarter angle.`
Two fingertips on each piece bring them together — fingertip contact only, never a full-hand grip — a visible snap or seat completes the join, fingertips withdraw. Restate the comparator (§Scale continuity anchor) in this shot's sentence; grip shots are where scale drifts most.

**8 HERO STAND / DETAIL PASS** · mandatory, always the last shot · 1.5-2.5 s
`Static 45-degree hero close-up.`
The assembled product stands or rests on the tabletop; if it's touched at all, only two fingertips pinch it lightly to tilt it — never a whole-hand hold — to catch the light across a signature detail (port, seam, sole, logo). Restate the comparator here too. Ends on a held static beat — never a new camera position.

## 📏 Scale continuity anchor — repeat it every shot, not just once

**Stating the scale ratio once in the style paragraph is not enough to stop the product drifting bigger or smaller between hard cuts.** Each shot is generated somewhat independently, and without a repeated concrete size cue, a tighter or wider framing on the next cut gets misread as the object itself growing or shrinking — the single most common continuity failure in test runs (the box reads matchbox-sized in shot 1, then noticeably larger by shot 3 with no in-story reason).

The fix: pick ONE concrete real-world comparator sized to match the product's stated scale — a coin, a matchbox, a fingertip's width, a grain of rice — and **repeat that same comparator phrase in every single shot's sentence**, not just in the style paragraph. Example comparator phrases: `no bigger than a coin` · `matchbox-sized` · `no wider than a fingertip` · `smaller than a grain of rice`.

- Pick the comparator once per reel, matching the scale already chosen (§Scale reference library), and use the identical phrase — not a paraphrase — in every shot.
- When a shot is a tighter macro than the previous one, say so explicitly as a lens/distance change, not a size change: `the lens closer now, the box itself still no bigger than a coin`. Never let framing alone imply the object grew.
- This applies to the product AND to any accessory that appears — the controller, cable, or blister pack gets its own comparator too if it's small enough to need one (e.g. `the cable no thicker than a strand of hair`).

**Grip shots are the highest-risk moment for scale drift.** The moment a hand actually holds or grips an item (not just resting near it), the model's learned priors pull toward normal handheld-object proportions — the item quietly grows to "comfortable grip size" even when every other shot stayed tiny. Counter this specifically whenever a shot involves picking up, holding, or manipulating the product or an accessory by hand:
- Specify **fingertip-only contact, never a whole-hand wrap or a palm grip**. `held pinched between just two fingertips` reads as tiny; `held in the hand` or `gripped` invites the model to scale it to a normal handheld size.
- Restate the comparator explicitly inside that shot's action, not just the framing: `held between two fingertips, the controller no bigger than a fingernail, dwarfed against the surrounding hand`.
- If the product is genuinely too small to be "held" at all without disappearing, prefer showing it resting on a fingertip or being nudged/tilted with a tool instead of picked up.

## 🔗 Object provenance rule

**Nothing may appear in a shot unless an earlier shot showed where it came from.** This is the single most common failure mode in test runs: a controller, cable or accessory shows up already free-standing or already in its blister pack with no prior shot of it being lifted from the box. Before finalising a shot list, trace every object backward: for each item that appears in shots 3 onward, find the earlier shot that shows it still inside the box or tray. If there isn't one, insert a TRAY / LINER PULL shot (or fold the reveal into an existing shot's exit action) before it appears.

## ✋ Hands and 🛠 tools bank

Hands: `Fingers reaching down from the top of frame` *(default entry, best for the anticipation/reveal beat, and for the big coarse moves — lifting the box, holding/tilting the finished product)* · `A thumb and forefinger` · `Both hands` · `One hand` · `An off-frame hand`. Wrists and below only — never sleeves, cuffs, rings, watches, nail polish, or any arm above the wrist.

Tools: `fine-tipped tweezers` — near-default, used for every fine unwrapping/extraction action (§Tweezer-first handling); include unless the product genuinely has nothing small enough to warrant it · `a hobby knife` (seal cuts only) · `a small screwdriver` (electronics with visible screws) · nothing else — don't invent tools a real unboxing wouldn't use.

---

## 🔊 Sound bank

Diegetic only, ordered to match the shot list. Draw from real per-archetype sounds, never a generic "unboxing sounds":

`the blade slicing through tape` · `the box lid creaking open` · `plastic film peeling back` · `the tray sliding out of the box` · `tweezers clicking against the blister pack` · `a small cable setting down on wood` · `two plastic parts snapping together` · `the product settling onto the tabletop`.

## 📏 Scale reference library

State the scale as a ratio or a plain-language size comparison — never leave it vague.

| Product category | Typical scale phrasing |
|---|---|
| Game console / set-top box | `roughly 1/6th of the real object's size` |
| Phone | `roughly the size of a matchbox` |
| Sneaker (single shoe) | `roughly the size of a thumb` |
| Small appliance (blender, toaster) | `roughly 1/10th of the real object's size` |
| Toy / collectible | `roughly half the size of the real toy` |

Whatever scale is chosen for the hero image must be repeated word-for-word in the video's style paragraph.

## ✍️ House spelling and punctuation

Timecodes take a plain ASCII hyphen and one decimal: `0:00.0-0:02.0`. Em dash before `SHOT n`. Never name an aspect ratio, orientation or resolution anywhere inside a prompt — scale phrasing and camera-angle words are the only controls. Never name an AI tool or platform inside a prompt body.
